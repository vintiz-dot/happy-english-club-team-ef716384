Play, Talk, Think

What the research says about turning Vietnamese 6-8-year-olds into confident English speakers and then writers, and how the new Level 1 and Level 2 lesson decks put it into practice.

The short answer

Very little research studies exactly our children: 6-8-year-olds learning English as a foreign language for three hours a week. Most of the strong evidence comes from children learning in their first language, or from adults. So the recommendations below combine the few strong findings with sensible design that is consistent with them, and say which is which.

Six things the evidence supports best

  1. Spaced retrieval. Bring words back at the start of every lesson, and again days and weeks later. Of everything here, this has the strongest evidence. Strong
  2. Talk with feedback. Pair work with a real reason to speak, and quick corrections that push the child to fix the mistake themselves (a prompt) rather than the teacher just repeating it correctly (a recast). Younger learners benefit more from feedback. Moderate-strong
  3. Guided, never unguided. Play and inquiry work when the adult holds a clear goal and steers. Leaving children to discover on their own does worse than teaching. Strong-moderate
  4. Words with actions. Acting out a word's meaning helps young children learn and remember concrete vocabulary. Moderate
  5. Chunks and repeated tasks. Teach useful phrases as wholes. Repeat a speaking task with new partners to build fluency, with one feedback moment in between so mistakes don't set. Moderate
  6. Systematic phonics with known words. Short, explicit letter-sound work, using words the children already understand. Strong in L1

Songs, puppets, role play, Frayer boxes, sentence frames, translanguaging and the concept-based inquiry model as a package all have weak direct evidence. None shows harm. They are good ways to deliver the six points above, so the decks use them that way, never as the objective itself.

What this means for a 90-minute lesson, twice a week

A lesson is a run of short blocks (5-15 minutes) that switch between moving, talking, sitting and making. Each block has one language goal. A suggested shape (the timings are our design, not taken from a study):

A. From fluent English users to English language arts

Input, interaction and output Moderate

Krashen's comprehensible input is now read as "necessary but not enough": children also need to speak and get feedback. Mackey & Goo's meta-analysis found clear gains from conversational interaction, smaller in classrooms than in labs. Lyster & Saito (15 classroom studies) found prompts tended to beat recasts, and younger learners gained more from feedback. Swain's output hypothesis is plausible, and direct tests are mixed.

In class: stay mostly in English with pictures, gestures and routines; end every input block with children saying something; ask for a full sentence and "why?"; correct with a quick, kind prompt ("He ___?").

Task-based teaching Moderate

Bryfonski & McKay's meta-analysis reported a large effect (d = 0.93). A 2025 reanalysis puts it nearer g = 0.61, and few of the studies involve young children. Shintani's work with 6-year-old beginners used listen-and-do tasks before speaking tasks.

In class: brief teaching of the target language, then a task with a real outcome (find the difference, sort, guess who, class survey). With beginners, start with listen-and-do.

Oracy and dialogic talk Weak for EFL

Mercer's "exploratory talk" (giving reasons, building on each other) improved children's reasoning in first-language studies. Voice 21's framework has four strands: physical, linguistic, cognitive, and social-emotional. The EEF's trial of dialogic teaching was rated low-to-moderate in security, and teachers found it hard to put in place.

In class: teach the talk moves as chunks ("I think… because…", "I agree with ___ because…", "Can you say more?") and expect heavy scaffolding at these levels.

Chunks, fluency, then accuracy and complexity Moderate

Formulaic sequences are a base for later grammar. The 4/3/2 technique (the same talk to three listeners, faster each time) builds fluency but not accuracy. Tran & Saito found that adding delayed feedback improved both. "Fluency, then accuracy, then complexity" is a useful teaching rule of thumb, not a proven sequence.

In class: "speed-date" rounds with the same picture or story, and one teacher feedback moment between the first and last round.

Vocabulary depth Moderate

Beck, McKeown & Kucan's robust vocabulary instruction (many meetings with a word, in varied contexts) comes from first-language classrooms. For our learners, everyday words are themselves new, so they are the target at Levels 1-2. A few "school" words (compare, because, same, different) start in Level 2 and grow toward ELA.

Phonics and early literacy Strong in L1

The National Reading Panel found systematic phonics helped beginning readers (about d = 0.41, more when started early); later reanalyses give smaller effects. The EEF's Key Stage 1 guidance puts speaking and listening first, alongside phonics and comprehension. Vietnamese uses the Latin alphabet, so letter shapes carry over. English final consonants, clusters and vowel contrasts need explicit practice.

Shared reading, oral rehearsal, sentence-level writing Moderate / weak

Dialogic reading (PEER: prompt, evaluate, expand, repeat) helps oral language and narrative (a 2026 meta-analysis found g = 0.62 for narrative skills). Sentence combining has a 0.50 effect in only five studies with older students. The Writing Revolution's evidence comes mostly from the programme itself.

In class: say the sentence to a partner, say it to the teacher, then write it. In Level 1, writing means tracing, copying, labelling and frames. In Level 2, children join sentences with and / but / because, first aloud, then on paper.

Retrieval, spacing and feedback Strong

Cepeda et al. (317 experiments) and Dunlosky et al. rate spaced practice and practice testing as the most useful study techniques. Hattie & Timperley show that feedback matters a great deal, and that its form decides whether it helps. "Here is what to do next" beats "good job".

Vietnamese as a resource Weak as a method

Translanguaging is supported mainly by qualitative work. Cummins' interdependence idea (skills in the first language support the second) is better grounded. In class: short, planned Vietnamese moments (check a word, rehearse an idea, compare a pattern), with English as the working language. Each grammar slide in the decks has one "Vietnamese tip".

BICS and CALP: the bridge to ELA Moderate as an idea

Cummins separates everyday conversational English from academic English (describe, compare, explain why, sequence). Children who stop at chat struggle when they reach ELA. So from Level 2 the decks add "school talk" with graphic organisers: sort, compare, give a reason.

B. Play-based learning (Level 1's core)

Guided play, not free play Moderate

In guided play, children have choice and the adult sets the goal and scaffolds (Weisberg, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff). Skene et al.'s 2022 meta-analysis (17 studies, ages 1-8) found guided play beat direct instruction on early maths (g = 0.24) and shapes (g = 0.63), with no difference on literacy or vocabulary. Most studies were at high risk of bias, and none involved English as a foreign language. The fair claim is "at least as effective as teaching, and more enjoyable", not "proven better".

Test for any activity: who is deciding? If the teacher gives every answer, it is no longer guided play.

The five characteristics Weak-moderate, as a checklist

Zosh et al. (LEGO Foundation): joyful, meaningful, actively engaging, iterative, socially interactive. They are useful for auditing an activity: aim for three or more in each activity and all five across a lesson.

Movement and TPR Moderate for concrete words

Children who acted out new words in exercise remembered them best, six weeks later too (Mavilidi et al., preschoolers). The TPR meta-analysis effect is large but very imprecise. Active lessons raise enjoyment and on-task behaviour (Watson 2017; Bedard 2019). Tie the action to the meaning, then move from doing to saying within the same lesson.

Songs, games, role play, stories, puppets Weak

Hamilton et al.'s 2024 review of 60 song studies found mostly positive results, but none strong enough to show the songs caused them, and no harm. Digital games show moderate effects for language. Role play and drama have weak causal evidence (Lillard 2013; Mages 2008). Use them as vehicles with a required phrase: "you must say ___ to get a card". Avoid elimination games, where the weakest children stop speaking.

Assessing play Weak (no method compared)

Write each objective as an observable "I can…". Carry a short checklist with 3-4 target behaviours, mark 4-6 children a lesson so everyone is seen once a week, and note one quote in the child's own words. A 2-minute "show me" at the end of a play block turns play into evidence.

C. Play blended with concept-based inquiry (Level 2's core)

Guidance is what makes inquiry work Strong-moderate

Alfieri et al. (164 studies): unassisted discovery did worse than explicit teaching (d = -0.38), while discovery with feedback, worked examples and prompts to explain did better (d = +0.30). Lazonder & Harmsen (72 studies): guidance helps at every age, and younger learners need more specific guidance. Klahr & Nigam warn against leaving 8-year-olds alone with materials.

Comparison, sorting and non-examples Moderate

Comparing examples helps children learn relational structure (Christie & Gentner). Concept attainment ("Is / Is Not", Bruner) and grouping-and-naming (Taba) are its classroom forms. Near-miss non-examples carry the most information. Frayer boxes and Hawkins' "messing about" have weak direct evidence in EFL but fit this pattern.

The inquiry phases Weak as a package

Marschall & French's phases (Engage, Focus, Investigate, Organize, Generalize, Transfer, Reflect) build on Erickson and Lanning's structure: facts lead to concepts, and concepts lead to generalizations (sentences linking two or more concepts). No controlled study shows the package improves transfer for primary children. Its parts (guidance, comparison, explaining why, testing a new case) are better supported. So the decks treat it as a well-designed lesson shape, not as proven.

Generalizing with frames, at age 7-8

  • One concept and one generalization per lesson, never more.
  • Teach the words and the frame first, in under 10 minutes; then investigate.
  • The frame stays the same for weeks and fades: "All ___ have ___." "Some ___, but not all." "___ because ___." "I think ___ because ___."
  • The teacher plans the generalization and never hands it over. Children say their own, and "Not always!" is welcome.
  • Transfer is its own step, with new examples the children have not seen.
  • Questions: factual for quick checks, one conceptual question per lesson, and one playful debatable question per unit ("Which is the best ___? Why?").
  • Keep the thinking bigger than the language. Children may notice and guess in Vietnamese; the final frame is in English.

How the Level 1 and Level 2 decks put this in place

Every deck, both levels

  • Opens with an original chant or game that brings back the last lesson's words (retrieval).
  • Two or more pair or group talk episodes, with frames on the board and a fluency tip.
  • The exact Student Book pages, activity letters and track numbers (play the tracks from your own copy), and the exact Workbook pages as homework.
  • A creative homework with a higher-order twist (invent, imagine, choose and say why) and a Challenge for high achievers.
  • A grammar slide for young children, after they have used the pattern in a game: a hook, the pattern, what it means, when to use it, a Vietnamese tip, and common mistakes.
  • A vlog prompt once per unit at Support, Core and Stretch.
  • Writing in the order: say it, then write it.

Level 1 (age 6-7, Pre A1 to A1)

Each lesson is a sequence of guided-play episodes: chant, TPR, vocabulary game, story or role play, the book page, a movement break, a speaking game, then a calm close. Every episode has one language goal. Grammar appears as a pattern in play, and writing starts with tracing, copying, labelling and frames.

Level 2 (age 7-8, A1 to A1+)

Play continues, and the inquiry phases are added at a child's level. Engage with a game or mystery bag; Focus with Is / Is Not and picture sorts; Investigate through the reading; Organize on a class chart; Generalize with frames; Transfer to a new case; Reflect with "I used to think… now I think…". Each unit lists the teacher's planned generalizations. They are never shown to children as answers.

Limits of this report

  • Almost none of the evidence is about 6-8-year-olds learning English in Vietnam. Most is about preschool or first-language children, or adults.
  • Several play sources are linked to the LEGO Foundation, which funded the Skene review team.
  • The research agents opened some sources in full. Others they confirmed only from abstracts or search summaries, and EEF pages were blocked. Check a source before quoting it to parents.
  • If you want real proof for HEC, add one transfer item at the end of each unit and compare classes over a term.
